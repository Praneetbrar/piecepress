**Piece Press answers one buyer question about a product, including when the answer is no.** A teammate who was not in the meeting should be able to open the URL and decide. [piecepress.com](https://piecepress.com) publishes those explainers. “Everything about Fieldnote” is a brochure. “Is Fieldnote worth adding on top of the help desk we already run?” is a question a page can finish.

## The test of a page you can forward

The recipient does not have your pitch. They have the link and a minute. The first section has to restate the product, the person, and the constraint without “as we discussed.”

Four things should be findable without an outline:

1. The question.
2. The person, with a real constraint: team size, a tool they already pay for, a deadline.
3. The tradeoff. What the product refuses, next to what that person gains.
4. The no. Who should keep the current setup.

If one of the four is missing, the URL is not ready to send as a decision. [How to write a decision explainer a buyer can send to a teammate](https://piecepress.com/blog/how-to-write-a-decision-explainer) is the full shape. The [decision outline](https://piecepress.com/tools/decision-outline) holds the skeleton — short answer, definition, tradeoff, “not this,” next step — and leaves the facts to you.

## Worth it compared with what

Worth is a comparison. Name the baseline in the first section: the shared inbox, the spreadsheet, the help desk, the Monday meeting. “The old way” is not a baseline.

Separate price from cost. Price is the invoice. Cost is the hour a lead spends switching, the ticket handled twice during the move, the report a director loses. A page that only argues the invoice is cheaper has not met the person who has to switch.

Use numbers you can point at. “Saves hours” is a mood. “Replaces a 20-minute standup read for a queue of 40” is a job with a count. Do not invent a percentage to look rigorous. [How to answer “is this product worth it?”](https://piecepress.com/blog/how-to-answer-is-this-product-worth-it) covers the baseline, the cost, and the no.

A sentence that can be pasted into chat comes after the argument, and it has to stand alone:

> Worth it for a support lead who already has a help desk and wants a morning brief, not a new queue.

If that sentence cannot stand alone, the article has not decided.

## What gets cited, and what gets skipped

People paste one sentence. Answer engines lift one sentence. The previous paragraph does not come with it.

“This means support leads can skip the Monday readout” fails, because “this” has no subject. “Fieldnote is a morning brief on top of an existing help desk, not a replacement queue” can be lifted. Praise cannot. “Powerful platform for modern teams” fits any product. A boundary fits one.

About 12 to 32 words is a useful range. Shorter is usually a slogan. Longer is usually two facts welded with “and.” The [citation line finder](https://piecepress.com/tools/citation-lines) lists sentences in that band that read like a definition, a boundary, or a number. [How to write a sentence worth citing](https://piecepress.com/blog/how-to-write-a-sentence-worth-citing) is the note behind it.

Put one precise sentence where a skimmer stops: the first paragraph, the tradeoff, or the “not this.” A precise sentence that lives only in paragraph eleven will not be the one that travels.

## Length, and the second question

Long enough for the question, the fit, the tradeoff, and the no. Often past 800 words. Rarely useful past 2,000 if a second question has crept in. A clear short no can be finished. A long feature tour can still be empty. The [explainer length](https://piecepress.com/tools/explainer-length) check is about room, not about a ranking score.

A second question — enterprise pricing, a migration from a different tool — is a second page. One URL, one decision. When a fact changes, edit this URL. Date a price with the month you checked it. If the answer itself flips, say so at the top. A quiet reversal is how the next teammate stops trusting the link they were sent.

## Questions about product explainers

### How long should a “worth it” article be?

As long as the no and the tradeoff need. Word count is not the point. A page of benefits with no refusal is a sales page, however long it is.

### Can I include a customer story?

Yes, if it answers the same question. An anecdote that changes the subject belongs on another URL.

### What if I have not used the product?

Say so. You can explain what the docs claim. Do not write the yes in the voice of a user you are not.

### Should competitors be named?

When the decision is “this, or the tool they already have.” Name that alternative and the criterion that separates them. A drive-by list of logos is a different form.

## One question, written out

Question: is Fieldnote worth adding for a support lead who already runs a help desk?

Person and constraint: a lead with a team of ten, a help desk they will not replace this quarter, and a Monday meeting that currently starts with someone reading the queue out loud.

Definition, the kind of sentence that can travel: Fieldnote is a morning brief on top of an existing help desk, not a replacement queue.

Tradeoff: the lead gets six lines before standup and gives up the hope that this tool will also be the system of record. It will not assign seats, rebuild reports, or become the place tickets live. It reads what the help desk already knows.

No: not worth it if the team has no help desk yet, or if the Monday meeting is already short because the queue is small. Also not worth it if the lead needs the brief to include calls and chat that never become tickets. The page should say that limit next to the benefit, not in a joke FAQ at the bottom.

Next step: run last week’s tickets through the brief, or keep reading the queue aloud. One link, in a sentence that matches the answer. If the answer for this reader is no, the link can still exist for the lead it is for. The page should not beg.

That is one piece. Enterprise procurement, a migration off a different desk, and a comparison of five briefs are other pieces. Putting them here would make the forwarded URL answer none of the questions cleanly.

## What the teammate does with the link

They open it on a phone, in a thread, between other messages. They will not read a feature table first. They will look for a sentence they can reply with. Write that sentence on purpose, near the top, after you have earned it.

A reply that helps the sender sounds like: “Only if we keep the current help desk — it will not replace it.” A reply that means the page failed sounds like: “What is this?” or “Is this an ad?” The second reply shows up when the page withheld the boundary, or when every paragraph could have been pasted onto a competitor.

Read the draft in a clean window, without the outline beside you. Find the question, the person, the tradeoff, and the no. If you have to scroll past a company story to reach the no, move the no up. Skeptical teammates look for it. They trust a page that can say no in the same glance as the yes.

## Costs that are not on the invoice

Price is the easiest number and often the least important one in the first month. The costs that decide “worth it” are usually time and risk. Who has to learn it. What breaks on the morning you switch. Which report a director loses. Which queue gets handled twice while the old tool and the new one both feel official.

Pick one of those and be concrete. If you do not know it, say what you checked and what you could not see. Inventing a painless setup is how these articles lose readers who have shipped a tool before. They are not asking for cheer. They are asking what the first week is like for the person in the question.

A sourced small number beats a round large one. “The brief is six lines” can be checked against the product. “Saves 40% of support time” cannot, unless you show the task, the team, and how you measured it. Naked statistics look like citations and behave like ads. Careful readers drop them. So do careful answer systems.

## Sentences that survive being quoted

Write the definition so it does not depend on a heading. “The second option is cheaper” means nothing in a quote. “The morning brief is cheaper than a second help desk seat for a team of ten, and it does not replace the desk” might. The subject is inside the sentence. The boundary is inside the sentence.

Avoid openings that cannot be pasted: “we think,” “it’s no secret,” “in today’s landscape,” “game-changer.” A person will not put those in a decision thread. A system that quotes them looks unreliable, so it will look for a better sentence, often on someone else’s page.

You do not need every line to be quotable. You need a few that carry the decision: what it is, what it refuses, who should walk away. The rest can be steps, examples, and the month you checked a price. A page of slogans has nothing to lift. A page with three precise sentences has a spine.

Place those sentences where a skimmer lands. If the only precise line is late, move it up and let the later paragraphs unpack it. The [citation line finder](https://piecepress.com/tools/citation-lines) will not tell you the sentence is true. It will tell you whether the draft has any line in a shape someone can lift. Truth and a source are still your job.

## Keeping the URL honest over time

Prices and plan names should carry the month you checked them. When they change, edit the sentence. The question in the title can stay, because the decision’s shape is stable even when the invoice moves. If the answer flips — the product now wants to be the help desk, not a brief on top of one — say that at the top of the same URL. People forwarded the old link. They should meet the new fact at that address, not a cheerful page that quietly means the opposite.

Do not retire the URL when a campaign ends. A sales page can disappear. An explainer that people used to decide should remain, corrected. Two contradictory pages about the same question, with no link between them, are how a search result and a chat quote disagree. One address, one current answer.

## More questions

### Where should the “not this” section sit?

Near the tradeoff, not under a pile of features and not as a fake objection. If the no changes the decision, it belongs where a skeptical reader looks before they decide to trust the yes.

### Can the piece recommend a trial?

Yes, as the next step, in a sentence that matches the answer. “Try last week’s tickets if you already have a help desk” is a trial tied to the question. “Start your free forever growth journey” is a banner. The first one helps the teammate. The second one marks the page as something else.

### What if the product is not worth it for the person in the question?

Write the no and stop. A clear no is a finished article. A hedged yes that hides the no is not an explainer. It is a pitch with a question in the title. Piece Press will publish the no. The page is for the reader, including the reader who should walk away.

### How do I know a second question has crept in?

When a section would still make sense if you deleted the question in the title. Enterprise security, a full migration, and a ranking of competitors often fail that test. Move them. The title’s question should be able to govern every section that remains.
