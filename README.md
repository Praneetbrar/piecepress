Piece Press publishes longer decision explainers at [piecepress.com](https://piecepress.com). Each piece answers one buyer question about a product: who it is for, what they give up, and when it is the wrong call. A teammate who opens the URL without the original conversation should still be able to decide.

Readers use a Piece Press page when a tagline is not enough and a shortlist is the wrong shape — one product, one question, enough depth to cite. Makers use it when the product needs a real boundary in public, not a list of features.

## What is a decision explainer?

A decision explainer is a page with a question it can finish. “Is Fieldnote worth adding on top of a help desk we already run?” can be finished. “Everything about Fieldnote” cannot. The title or the first line states the question. The body answers it with a definition, a tradeoff, a “not this,” and a next step.

Length is room for the no. A long page that never refuses anything is a sales page with extra sections. A shorter page that includes the boundary can be finished. Most useful pieces sit between those: long enough to hold the decision, short enough that a second question is split into its own URL.

## Who it is for

**Buyers and the teammates they forward to.** The recipient was not in the meeting. The page has to restate the product, the person, and the constraint without “as we discussed.”

**Makers** whose product is easier to trust once the refusal is written down. What it will not replace. Who should keep the current tool. Which cost is not the invoice.

**People who cite pages** — a colleague in chat, a search result, an answer engine. They lift one sentence. That sentence has to name the subject and stay true out of context.

If the product can be explained in a sharp opening, [Hed Press](https://hedpress.com) is the better desk. If the reader is comparing several named options, [Listed Press](https://listedpress.com) is the better desk. [Lede Press](https://ledepress.com) is the daily index of shorter product stories.

## What a reader gets

- One question, visible at the start, so they know what the page is deciding.
- A baseline: worth it compared with the inbox, the spreadsheet, or the help desk they already pay for. “The old way” is not a baseline.
- A cost that is not only the sticker price — the hour to switch, the report someone loses, the seat they do not need.
- A section that starts with who should not use the product, near the tradeoff, not hidden as a fake FAQ (“not for people who hate saving time”).
- One sentence a teammate can paste. “Worth it for a support lead who already has a help desk and wants a morning brief, not a new queue.”
- A URL that stays. When a fact changes, the page is updated. The link people already forwarded still lands on the decision.

## What makes a sentence worth citing

A citable sentence names the subject, states a checkable fact or a boundary, and still makes sense alone. About 12 to 32 words is a useful range.

“This means support leads can skip the Monday readout” fails alone, because “this” has no subject. “Fieldnote is a morning brief on top of an existing help desk, not a replacement queue” can be lifted.

Praise does not travel. “Powerful platform for modern teams” fits any product. A boundary fits one. Numbers need a noun and a trail: “replaces a 20-minute standup read for a 40-ticket queue,” not “saves 40%.” Put at least one precise sentence where a skimmer lands — the first paragraph, the tradeoff, or the “not this” — not only in paragraph eleven.

## How a worth-it piece stays honest

Worth is a comparison. Name the baseline in the first section and attach every claim to it. Separate price from cost. If you did not run the workflow, say so. Do not write the yes in the voice of a user you are not.

Date prices and plan names with the month you checked them. If the answer itself flips, say so at the top of the same URL. A quiet reversal is how a site loses the next teammate.

End on the next step, not a crescendo. Try the workflow, or keep the current tool. One link, in a sentence that matches the answer. If the answer was no, the link can still exist for the person it is for.

## What we will not publish

- Two buyer questions on one URL.
- A “not this” written so no real person fits it.
- Invented percentages, customers, or setup times.
- A page that misstates the product or sends the reader somewhere else.
- A feature tour that never says who should walk away.

## How to read a piece before you forward it

You should be able to find four things without the outline: the question, the person, the tradeoff, and the no. If one is missing, do not send the URL as a decision. Open it again after a fact you care about would have changed, and see whether the page moved or went stale in silence.

## Questions

### How long should an explainer be?

Long enough for the question, the fit, the tradeoff, and the no. Often past 800 words. Rarely useful past 2,000 if a second question has crept in. Word count is not the point. The no is.

### Should there be a star rating?

No. A star hides the baseline. The quotable sentence is the rating.
